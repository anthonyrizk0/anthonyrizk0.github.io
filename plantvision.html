PlantVision

Real-time, on-device plant identification on iOS — a two-stage detector/classifier pipeline running under ARKit, shipped to the App Store at a fifteen-person startup, before on-device ML was something you could assume.

Greg (greg.app) · Aug 2021 – Feb 2023 · Lead Software Engineer, iOS
Stack Swift · ARKit · Core ML · YOLOv5 · fine-tuned MobileNet · vDSP/Accelerate

The bet

Greg already had plant identification, through a third-party server-side API. It worked — but it was discrete, latency-heavy, network-dependent and expensive at scale.

The hypothesis was that identification could become a primary interaction loop — continuous and ambient rather than a utility you occasionally invoke. That required real-time, which required on-device. The investment case was broader than the feature: App Store visibility, investment signal, business development. Apple has since built substantially this capability into the native camera, which both validates the direction and is a lesson about timing.

I led the iOS implementation of the pipeline and the test harness, and co-designed the architecture with our CTO. Mine specifically: the crop/classification flow, ARKit integration, scheduling behaviour, performance work, and the shipped angle-guidance experience.

Architecture

The key insight was that classification shouldn't be a single-shot event. It's incremental evidence accumulation across frames. A single pass per detection was noisy — flickering labels, oscillating confidence — and that one decision is what drove stability, the scheduling model, and an AR interaction that felt continuous rather than strobing.

The constraint was sustained throughput, not a per-frame deadline. Inference ran asynchronously from the render loop; we weren't trying to classify every frame. Fewer passes just meant slower convergence and a less responsive feel. The unit of work stopped being "a frame" and became "a tracked candidate that needs more evidence."

Four tradeoffs that shaped it

1 · On-device vs server-side

The existing API was accurate but discrete and costly at scale. On-device meant zero marginal cost, real-time, offline-capable — trading raw accuracy ceiling for interaction quality. This single decision unlocked everything else, including a dividend nobody planned for.

2 · The frame-buffer crop

Passing only YOLO's bounding-box region into MobileNet rather than the full frame. Critical to throughput — the classifier saw a smaller, more relevant input. Alongside it: the classifier's output is a large float vector, and the naive argmax loop was a genuine bottleneck. Moving it to vDSP gave at least an order of magnitude.

3 · Stability vs responsiveness

Temporal smoothing and confidence aggregation across passes, tuned empirically through the test harness rather than derived from first principles. Pass frequency against per-pass quality was a recurring tension, not a setting we solved once.

4 · Training-data bias correction

Community-sourced training data over-represented common species, so the classifier over-predicted them in the wild. Our ML engineer identified it; rather than retraining, we applied per-species calibration after the raw output. Pragmatic, and it measurably improved real-world quality.

Under the hood

Tracking

Once YOLO found a plant we had to maintain its identity across frames, or every detection would be a disconnected event and the overlays would jitter. ARKit's camera pose gave world-space continuity; on top of it we wrote association logic over projected positions and temporal consistency. Each tracked candidate carried its own state — world position, screen region, classification history, confidence, temporal metadata. Effectively lightweight multi-object tracking under constrained compute.

Scheduling

With several candidates competing for limited throughput, we evolved a prioritisation model over recency, confidence and screen visibility. That scheduler was not designed up front. It emerged from watching the system behave and iterating. In retrospect the architecture had become a lightweight inference scheduler over tracked candidates — which is not how anyone would have described it at the start.

The test harness

A separate internal app consuming the same shared framework, with the parameter surface exposed: model selection, thresholds, detection sensitivity, pass frequency, candidate count. The point was that anyone on the team, engineer or not, could run controlled experiments across devices. Nearly every empirical decision above came out of it.

What to surface

The system could do more than the product should. Internal builds supported multiple simultaneous identifications, continuous overlays and persistent AR anchors — compelling in a demo, and in testing consistently overwhelming. Overlays competed for attention, confidence updates read as visual noise, and the whole thing felt unstable even when it was correct.

And instant turned out to be too instant. Identification that resolved before the user had asked for it read as the system doing something to them rather than for them; people preferred to feel in control of when it fired. Tap-to-identify solved both at once — the prompt appears once we have confidence, and the decision to take it stays the user's.

What shipped, and what we never found out

Shipped: App Store consumer release, real-time AR identification, angle guidance, zero-cost community photo labelling, and an internal test harness the team actually used.

The thing that actually hit us was Apple's App Tracking Transparency change, which inverted CAC against LTV and made paid acquisition uneconomical. That's the measurable event, it applied to the whole company rather than to PlantVision specifically, and it's why the window closed.

What PlantVision itself did is genuinely unresolved. We didn't see a large uptick after launch, and we also didn't have long enough to know whether one was coming.

I want to be exact about that, because there's a tidier version of this story — "the hypothesis was wrong, identification turned out to be episodic rather than habit-forming" — and I've caught myself telling it that way. It's a plausible hypothesis. It isn't something we established. A confident post-hoc explanation for an inconclusive outcome is exactly the kind of thing worth distrusting.

In retrospect we should have shipped something simpler, faster, which would have bought precisely the time we ran out of. I had high conviction on this work, and some of that conviction was wanting to build with genuinely interesting technology. From the inside those two are easy to confuse.

What carries forward technically: two-stage on-device inference pipelines, incremental classification over time, lightweight multi-object tracking, async scheduling under constrained compute. What carries forward as judgment: acquisition beats activation, and user experience beats technology — both things I'd have said I believed at the time, and was in practice weighting the other way.

The broader lesson is the one I keep returning to: successful AI product work is usually less about maximising model capability than about shaping interaction around a system that is imperfect and always will be. Building something technically impressive is table stakes.
