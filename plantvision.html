PlantVision

Real-time, on-device plant identification on iOS — a two-stage detector/classifier pipeline running under ARKit, using Core ML and our own fine tuned model

Greg (greg.app) · Aug 2021 – Feb 2023 · Lead Software Engineer, iOS
Tech Stack Swift · ARKit · Core ML · YOLOv5 · fine-tuned MobileNet · vDSP/Accelerate

The bet

Greg already had plant identification, through a third-party server-side API. It worked — but it was discrete, latency-heavy, network-dependent and expensive at scale.

Our hypothesis was that real-time plant identification could do a few things:

  • 'Magical' first-run experience
  • Increase the chance you'd add a plant to the app (our key activation metric)
  • Cool feature for people to share/talk about the app
  • Provide better chance for app store visibility

Architecture

Classification was incremental ML prediction aggregated across frames

Under the hood

Tracking

Once YOLO found a plant we maintained its identity across frames, keeping track of classification history.

Scheduling

With several candidates competing for limited throughput, we evolved a prioritisation model over recency, confidence and screen visibility. That scheduler was not designed up front. It emerged from watching the system behave and iterating. In retrospect the architecture had become a lightweight inference scheduler over tracked candidates — which is not how anyone would have described it at the start.

Test harness

A separate internal app consuming the same shared framework, with the parameters exposed: e.g. model selection, thresholds, detection sensitivity, pass frequency, candidate count. Allowed anyone on the team, engineer or not, to run controlled experiments across devices.

What to surface

Our full stack supported multiple simultaneous almost instantaneous identifications, continuous overlays and persistent AR anchors — which was our initial vision, and looked very impressive but proved confusing for actual users, both in terms of too much information and lack of clarity about what they were supposed to do.

What shipped, and what we never found out

Shipped: App Store consumer release, real-time AR identification, angle guidance, zero-cost community photo labelling, and an internal test harness.

The thing that actually hit us was Apple's App Tracking Transparency change, which inverted CAC/LTV and made paid acquisition uneconomical.

The effect of PlantVision itself did was genuinely unresolved. We didn't see a large uptick after launch, and we also didn't have long enough to know whether one was coming.

I want to be exact about that, because there's a tidier version of this story — "the hypothesis was wrong, identification turned out to be episodic rather than habit-forming" — and I've caught myself telling it that way. It's a plausible hypothesis. It isn't something we established. A confident post-hoc explanation for an inconclusive outcome is exactly the kind of thing worth distrusting.

In retrospect we should have shipped something simpler, faster, which would have bought precisely the time we ran out of. I had high conviction on this work, and some of that conviction was wanting to build with genuinely interesting technology. From the inside those two are easy to confuse.

What carries forward technically: two-stage on-device inference pipelines, incremental classification over time, lightweight multi-object tracking, async scheduling under constrained compute. What carries forward as judgment: acquisition beats activation, and user experience beats technology — both things I'd have said I believed at the time, and was in practice weighting the other way.

The broader lesson is the one I keep returning to: successful AI product work is usually less about maximising model capability than about shaping interaction around a system that is imperfect and always will be. Building something technically impressive is table stakes.
